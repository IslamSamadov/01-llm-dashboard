What is an LLM?

LLM stands for Large Language Model — a type of artificial intelligence trained on vast amounts of text from books, websites, code, and other sources. During training, the model learns statistical patterns in language: which words and phrases tend to follow one another. At inference time, it does not “look up” answers in a database; instead it predicts the next token (small chunk of text) again and again, building a coherent reply one step at a time.

Because they are trained at scale, LLMs can draft emails, summarize documents, write code, translate languages, and hold multi-turn conversations — though they can also hallucinate facts and should be verified for high-stakes use.

Well-known examples include GPT-4o (OpenAI), used in ChatGPT and many APIs; Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic), known for long-context writing and analysis; and Gemini 1.5 Pro (Google), which supports very large context windows for document-heavy workflows.

What is a Token?

A token is the basic unit of text that an LLM reads and generates — not always a whole word. Tokenizers split text into chunks; on average, one token is roughly 3–4 characters or about ¾ of a word in English. Short common words may be one token; longer or rare words are often split into several tokens.

Tokens matter because providers bill by token count and cap how many tokens fit in a single request. The context window is the maximum number of input plus output tokens in one conversation; exceeding it means older messages must be dropped or summarized.

For example, the sentence "Hello, world!" might be tokenized roughly as: ["Hello", ",", " world", "!"] — four tokens depending on the model’s tokenizer.

English vs Azerbaijani: The same idea in English often uses fewer tokens than in Azerbaijani. Most tokenizers are trained heavily on English and Latin-script European languages, so English words frequently map to efficient, familiar subword units. Azerbaijani text — with agglutinative suffixes, Turkic vocabulary, and less representation in training data — tends to be split into more, smaller pieces. A short English prompt like "Summarize this article." might use about 5 tokens, while an equivalent Azerbaijani sentence such as "Bu məqaləni xülasə edin." can require noticeably more tokens for the same meaning. That means Azerbaijani users often pay more per message and fill the context window faster than English users for comparable content.
